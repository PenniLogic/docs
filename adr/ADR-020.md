## ADR-020 — Administrative boundary: separate deployable, database role and three audit streams
**Status:** ACCEPTED · 2026-09-30

**Decision.** The administrative surface is two separately deployed processes on administrative
hosts — the `admin` console (Next.js, holds no database credential) and the `admin-api` (a separate
build artefact of the `api` repository) — behind their own ingress and their own identity domain.
The admin API connects to Postgres as a dedicated `admin_app` role whose only path to customer data
is `EXECUTE` on audited `SECURITY DEFINER` functions in an `admin_api` schema, under the invariant that
no read result leaves the database before its recording event is durable; it holds no table
privilege on customer data, no `BYPASSRLS`, and never receives a data encryption key. Every
administrative route passes one default-deny policy evaluation point. Every action is exactly one
transactional write into the foundational audit trail carrying an `audit_event_id`; a relay projects
that write into the chained tamper-evident administrative log (its own cluster plus a write-once copy)
and into the subject-visible transparency stream (a positive projection with a closed vocabulary), and
an independent verifier confirms each chained record. No stream is dual-written. An unmasked value, an
elevated session, an export artefact, a production broker session or a break-glass session is released
only after the verifier has confirmed its chain record; otherwise it is refused, never compensated.
Controls are gated by capability, not by calendar phase (§9).

**Ticket.** `T-ADR-ADMIN-06`, PenniLogic/docs#40, parent epic E26 (docs#29). Consumed by `T-ADM-01`
to `T-ADM-13`, `T-SUP-01`, `T-SEC-03`, `T-NOT-01`, `T-PLT-05`, `T-CON-07`, `T-CON-09`, `T-TRU-02`
to `T-TRU-04` and `T-QA-13`. It builds on ADR-002 (append-only records), ADR-005 (default deny;
detail-level access to another person's data is logged and visible to them; reconciled in §5),
ADR-006 (per-user DEKs), ADR-009 (Kotlin/Ktor core, Postgres) and ADR-014 (`admin` is a separate
service on a separate auth domain). It coordinates by reference with ADR-018 (encryption scope, RLS
predicates, session context, key custody), ADR-019 (customer identity provider, passkeys, workload
identity, recovery), ADR-021 (shared-data erasure) and ADR-022 (runtime platform, AI egress). It
restates none of their internals.

### 1. Problem

The brief asks for an administrator who can "control the full application". Built literally, inside
the customer application, that makes every staff account — and every phished staff credential — a
total-compromise vector over other people's salary, debt and spending data, because the customer
process, database role, session cookie domain and deploy pipeline would all be shared. Monetary
columns are deliberately plaintext integers (ADR-001, ADR-002 constraints run in the database), so
any credential that reaches the tables reads every balance. The controls that would contain this are
described inconsistently across the research sources (§2), and three audit streams are named without
a stated relationship, store topology or write semantics. Without this record `T-SEC-03` cannot freeze
the audit event schema and `T-ADM-01` cannot start.

### 2. The inconsistency this record resolves

Four statements disagree about what must exist before the administrative surface is used:

| # | Source | Statement |
|---|---|---|
| (a) | `architecture/02-security-architecture.md` §11 "Security Requirements Checklist" | Lists ADMIN-01 (separate subdomain + auth domain), ADMIN-02 (redacted-by-default), ADMIN-03 (JIT elevation with ticket gating) and BACKEND-04 (hash-chained audit log streamed to write-once storage) under **MVP (Pre-Launch)**, and ADMIN-10 (four-eyes approval for bulk exports), ADMIN-11 (break-glass procedures documented and tested), ADMIN-12 (anomaly detection) and REBAC-11 (access transparency log visible to data subjects) under **Post-MVP / Series A** |
| (b) | `compliance/01-regulatory-landscape.md` §0 obligation matrix | O-22 "Administrative access lifecycle, approver eligibility and recertification" and O-23 "Support intake, ticket verification for unmasking, and the SLA" are gated **Before any unmask capability**; O-29 "No standing human production-data access and governed break-glass reads" is gated **Before real data** |
| (c) | `architecture/02-security-architecture.md` §2.2 | Principle 2 requires that "a second admin must approve" every JIT elevation and Principle 6 forbids approving one's own elevation — while §11 (a) ships elevation at MVP without its approver and defers four-eyes to Series A |
| (d) | `product/01-product-spec.md` §5 and `product/02-risk-register.md` R5 | The admin console is Phase 3 "Reach", "not parallel workstreams" — while (a) lists ADMIN-01 to ADMIN-03 as pre-launch MVP |

**Resolution.** Phases are delivery sequencing; controls are conditions of capabilities. This record
replaces the calendar phasing in (a) for everything administrative with the capability gates in §9:
the four-eyes control, the break-glass governance and the administrator lifecycle are conditions of
the capabilities they protect, not later hardening, and those capabilities do not exist until their
conditions do. Statement (b) already uses that framing and is confirmed. Statement (c) is resolved in
favour of §2.2: elevation without a distinct approver never exists. Statement (d) is compatible: the
console may be built in any phase, but whenever it is reachable with real data, Gate A (§9) applies.
None of the source documents is edited: the security architecture document's execution-gate banner
and ownership table already defer the administrative boundary to this record, and (b), (c) and (d)
need no deferral because they are confirmed or compatible as stated.

### 3. Deployment topology

#### 3.1 Chosen: separate deployables on administrative hosts

```
operator browser ──mTLS + allowlist──▶ admin console (Next.js, `admin` repo, host: admin zone)
                                             │  administrative contract only (T-CON-07)
                                             ▼
                                       admin-api (Ktor, `api` repo, own artefact, host: admin zone)
                                             │  role admin_app → EXECUTE on schema admin_api only
                                             ▼
                              primary Postgres ──── audit.event (trail = outbox) ◀── customer api
                                             │              │                 ▲
                                             │              │                 │ audit.chain_confirmed /
                                             │              │                 │ audit.approval_invalid /
                                             │              │                 │ audit.verification.*
                                             │       audit relay (platform zone)        │
                                             │       ├─ chain projector (chain_writer) ─┼─▶ audit_chain cluster ─▶ write-once copy
                                             │       └─ transparency projector ─▶ transparency.entry (+ notify outbox)
                                             │                                          │
                                             │       chain verifier (infrastructure zone, chain_verifier) ◀── credential registry (read-only)
                                             │
              redaction service (customer zone, holds KMS unwrap) ◀── internal unmask call by audit_event_id
```

1. **Two administrative processes, separate from every customer process.** The console is the
   `admin` repository's Next.js service (ADR-014; scaffolded deny-only by `T-SCA-ADM-01`,
   PenniLogic/admin#1, whose import boundary already refuses customer web code and whose request
   boundary denies everything until an administrative identity provider is configured). The admin
   API is an `admin-api` module of the `api` repository built into its **own artefact** with its own
   entrypoint and deployment manifest (`T-ADM-01`). The customer API artefact contains no
   administrative route and the administrative artefact contains no customer-facing route; both are
   proven by route-table absence tests (§13).
2. **Separate hosts and network segment.** Administrative processes run on hosts that run no
   customer workload, in an administrative network segment. Ingress is a separate load balancer and
   hostname that accepts only mutually authenticated connections (client certificate) from the
   allowlisted operator network; the edge also rejects the framework-answered request classes the
   admin scaffold documents (`TRACE`, `TRACK`, `CONNECT`, asterisk- and absolute-form targets,
   `Upgrade`). Egress from the admin zone is limited to: the admin API (from the console); the
   primary database `admin_api` schema and the chain cluster's `chain_reader` role (from the admin
   API); the primary database `audit.event` `idp.*` namespace (from the administrative identity
   provider's log forwarder as `idp_ingest`, §7); the administrative identity provider; the
   ticket-verification internal endpoint; and the internal unmask endpoint (§6). The
   ticket-verification interface (`T-SUP-01`) and the unmask endpoint are internal,
   workload-token-authenticated endpoints on listeners distinct from the customer-facing route table,
   and their caller is the admin API under its `admin-api` workload identity — never the console;
   PenniLogic/api#58's "the admin console calls" wording follows this record. These identities and
   audiences sit outside ADR-019 §13's four-audience enumeration, so **this record registers them
   under ADR-019 §13's audience and `typ` dispatch rule**: workload identity `admin-api` (issuer of
   `wl+jwt` only), audience `pennilogic-api-ticket-verify` for the ticket-verification listener and
   audience `pennilogic-redaction-unmask` for the unmask listener. The ticket-verification listener
   accepts a `wl+jwt` whose `iss` is `admin-api` and whose `aud` is its own value; the unmask listener
   accepts `iss` ∈ {`admin-api`, `export-job`} — the export workload identity of §5 item 5 is the one
   other permitted issuer, for that audience only — and nothing else; neither listener accepts an
   `at+jwt`, a `dlg+jwt` or the operator delegation token; customer endpoints reject both audiences
   (`AudienceSeparationTest` extended). There is no route from the admin zone to the customer-facing
   API, the AI service or the customer web application, and none from the customer zone to the admin
   zone. The infrastructure zone's verifier holds the only path to the administrative identity
   domain's read-only credential registry (§5 item 4); the customer zone has none.
3. **No shared process, session or cookie domain.** The administrative origin is a hostname no
   customer surface uses. Administrative session cookies carry the `__Host-` prefix (Secure,
   host-only, no `Domain` attribute) and `SameSite=Strict`, so no customer origin can set, read or
   ride them even if both surfaces sit under one registrable domain; the customer application may
   never set a cookie whose scope covers the administrative host. A separate registrable domain is
   permitted and preferred when available but is not a condition of this decision, so no domain
   purchase is decided here.
4. **Separate workload identities and secrets.** Each administrative process has its own platform
   identity. Administrative secrets are never mounted into customer workloads and vice versa. The
   administrative identities hold no KMS permission over customer key-encryption keys (§6.2).
5. **Runtime-neutral.** Which platform provides these hosts is ADR-022's runtime question. Any
   runtime chosen there must satisfy: no co-scheduling of administrative and customer workloads on
   one host; separately addressable network segments with default-deny rules between them; distinct
   workload identities; a separate deployment stage for the administrative artefacts; the chain
   store as a **separate PostgreSQL cluster** — its own hosts, superuser set, credentials, backups and
   WAL — administered under an identity that is neither the primary-cluster path nor the admin or
   relay identities, with its write-once copy in a third identity boundary (§8.1, §8.5); and data
   residency for logs as required by the counsel-reviewed jurisdiction profile (`T-CMP-04`; the
   research baseline is India-region storage for Indian users' logs). Every host that writes or
   verifies a stream synchronises to the time source the profile requires (§8.5).

#### 3.2 Rejected alternatives

| Alternative | Why rejected |
|---|---|
| **Administrative routes inside the customer API and web app**, guarded by a role claim | One remote-code-execution, SSRF, template or dependency flaw in the customer surface reaches administrative capability; the customer database role (or a superuser added "for admin") is shared, so a BOLA bug becomes a cross-tenant read; the session cookie domain and deploy pipeline are shared; the surface cannot be network-restricted without restricting customers; and the audit writer lives in the blast radius it audits. It is the total-compromise topology §1 describes |
| **Same artefact, separate host** (an `ADMIN_MODE` flag) | Administrative routes ship dormant in every customer instance; a misconfigured customer instance becomes an administrative one; route-table absence cannot be proven |
| **Console with direct database access** (a read-only replica credential in the Next.js server) | Monetary columns are plaintext integers by design, so "read-only" is a full financial read that bypasses redaction, the policy point and the audit write; forbidden outright (§4.1) |
| **Administrative API in the `admin` repository** (Next.js route handlers holding database credentials) | The console would hold production credentials; redaction and the `SECURITY DEFINER` functions belong beside the domain model in `api` |
| **Shared identity realm with an `is_admin` claim** | Customer recovery paths become administrative account-takeover paths; token replay across audiences; one phished customer-grade credential is an administrator (§7) |
| **Remote policy-decision service** in the request path | Adds an availability dependency in the deny path; unreachable means fail closed means outage. Policies are a versioned bundle evaluated in-process (§5) |

Rules: no login role used by any process is a superuser, a table owner or `BYPASSRLS`; the console
holds no database credential at all; **no production database access from the console** — the only
path from an operator to a row is console → admin API → `admin_api` function. **One write mechanism
for `audit.event`:** writers hold plain table `INSERT`, and the binding of each action namespace to
exactly one role is a row-level policy, not a function — `audit.event` has `FORCE ROW LEVEL SECURITY`
(owner `audit_owner`) and one `FOR INSERT TO <role> WITH CHECK (action LIKE '<namespace>.%')` policy
per writer: `admin.*` → `admin_api_owner`; `redaction.*` → `redaction_service`; `audit.*` →
`chain_verifier`; `idp.*` → `idp_ingest`; `broker.*` → the `T-PLT-05` broker identity; `auth.*`,
`consent.*`, `sharing.*` → `pennilogic_app`. There is no admitting policy for any other role and no
`INSERT` function, so the binding holds without any code path: the administrative process cannot
forge a chain confirmation, an approval verdict, a delivery record or a broker event, and the relay
cannot write a confirmation at all. The two `SECURITY DEFINER` status functions are owned by
`pennilogic_ctx`; the `admin_api` functions by `admin_api_owner`; no `audit.*` function has any other
owner and `audit_owner` owns none — the two definer-owner families ADR-018 §4.4 publishes, so its
conformance lint passes unchanged. Direct human access to the primary cluster, the chain cluster,
backups, object stores and key custodians is governed by `T-PLT-05` (empty standing access, brokered,
second-approved, time-boxed sessions that write into the same streams with the same
`audit_event_id`, and that open only under the release gate of §8.4). **A broker session's requester
and approver are administrative principals of §7** — device-bound credential, one person one
principal, lifecycle and recertification, `admin:hw_fresh` — never a platform or cloud IAM identity,
and the `broker.*` request and approval events carry those principal ids and the approver's signed
assertion, which the verifier checks exactly as it checks a console approval (§5 item 4). **No
brokered or break-glass human session ever includes KMS unwrap or decrypt permission, a wrapped-DEK
row together with custodian access, or plaintext key material**; key-custodian operations (rotation,
destruction, custodian migration) are a separate dual-control path under ADR-018 §6.1.1 that yields
no plaintext DEK or KEK to any human, and no human-assignable role or broker session template
carries a decrypt or unwrap permission (§13 item 2). Migrations, including partition detachment, run
under a separate migration role, serialized, one at a time, under the same governance.

#### 4.2 What "masked by default" means at the database

Masked views omit monetary and ciphertext columns rather than transforming them: an `admin_api` view
cannot select a balance, an amount, a merchant ciphertext or an account reference. They are
`security_invoker = true` views owned by `admin_api_owner` and readable by no login role; they exist
only to be consumed inside the definer functions of §4.3 after the event insert and
`begin_admin_access`, so a bare view read is impossible and ADR-018 §4.2's view lint passes
unchanged. The administrative contract (`T-CON-07`) therefore has no field in which an unmasked
financial value could travel by default; unmasking is a distinct operation (§6). Aggregate reads for
analytics (`T-ADM-10`) exist only as pre-defined `admin_api` stage-and-fetch pairs (§4.3) that return
cohort statistics with a minimum cohort size (§16) and never a per-subject row; they accept no ad-hoc
filter, and each function is reviewed for differencing risk before it is added.

#### 4.3 Rule for cross-tenant administrative reads

Every administrative read is cross-tenant. It is permitted only in these forms:

1. **Masked single-subject lookup**: one subject identified by an account identifier or by the
   subject's own support request; fields limited to the operator's role by policy; through the
   `admin_api` stage-and-fetch function pair below, which consumes a masked view; never a search or
   listing over financial fields. The console structurally has no route that lists an individual's
   transactions or balances (`T-ADM-04`).
2. **Unmasked read** of named fields of one subject, only through the approved unmask path (§6), with
   an eligible access basis, a recorded purpose, a distinct approver, a time-box and chain-before-
   plaintext.
3. **Aggregate read** through a pre-defined cohort function with the published minimum cohort size.
4. **Bulk export** never through the console; only a formally requested, dual-approved export job
   (`T-ADM-05`) whose output lands in a governed location and is itself recorded and subject-visible.

Cross-tenant reads happen inside `SECURITY DEFINER` functions owned by `admin_api_owner` under one
invariant: **no read result leaves the database before its recording event is durable.** A single
"insert the event, then read, then return" function is not enough — `BEGIN; SELECT admin_api.lookup(subject);
ROLLBACK;` would return the masked rows to the caller and leave no trail event, defeating §11 and the
committed-event cap. The read is therefore split into **record-and-stage** and **fetch-after-commit**:

1. `admin_api.lookup_stage(...)` — in one transaction, inserts the foundational event (§8.1), which
   fires the trigger that records the event and its subject; enters the administrative context through
   `begin_admin_access`; performs the read under `admin_recorded_access`; and writes the masked result
   into `admin_api.staged_result` — keyed to the `audit_event_id` and carrying the **operator principal
   and administrative session id** the event recorded, plus `expires_at` — under a `FOR INSERT` policy
   admitting only `admin_api_owner`. It returns **only the `audit_event_id`** — never a row. If the
   transaction rolls back, event and stage vanish together and nothing was disclosed.
2. `admin_api.lookup_fetch(audit_event_id, operator_principal, session_id)` — in a **later**
   transaction, verifies that the recording event is **committed**, where committed means exactly: the
   event row's inserting **top-level transaction has completed and committed** — never the current
   transaction and never one of its subtransactions. PostgreSQL shows a transaction its own
   uncommitted writes, and a `SAVEPOINT`/`RELEASE` pair changes the row's `xmin` to a subtransaction
   id, so mere visibility, or a naive `xmin <> pg_current_xact_id()` test, is not the check. The
   `pennilogic_ctx` status function `audit.event_committed(audit_event_id)` (reading under
   `audit_status_read`) returns true only when `pg_xact_status(xmin) = 'committed'`, `xmin` is not the
   current top-level transaction id and `xmin` is not a member of the current transaction's
   subtransaction set (`pg_current_xact_id()` and the subxact ids the session has opened); an event
   inserted by the calling transaction, by any of its savepoints, or by a rolled-back transaction is
   therefore **not committed** for this purpose, whatever the caller can see. On that condition, and
   in the **same `UPDATE` statement**, the fetch requires that the stage's recorded principal and
   session equal the caller's (`operator_principal`, `session_id`), that `expires_at >
   transaction_timestamp()`, and that `consumed_at IS NULL`; it marks the stage consumed and returns
   the masked rows — once. Any failed condition returns nothing and writes a chained
   `admin.lookup.refused` event with the reason (`not_committed`, `principal_mismatch`,
   `session_mismatch`, `expired`, `consumed`, `cap_exceeded`); the database-side distinct-subject
   cap of §11 is evaluated at fetch, and its refusal is recorded the same way. A stage that is never
   fetched is unfetchable from its `expires_at` (§16) onward by that predicate — the `admin_api`
   housekeeping purge is hygiene, not the control — and stage rows never enter any stream.

The same invariant governs every administrative disclosure: unmask plaintext is released only by the
redaction service after the verifier has confirmed the committed grant (§6.2, §8.4 item 3), export
plaintext only per subject through that path (§5 item 5), aggregate results only through the same
stage-and-fetch pair, and the database-side distinct-subject cap of §11 counts **committed** events —
which is exactly what fetch-after-commit makes correct. ADR-018 §5.5 owns the setter form this pair
uses and carries the matching sentence for the invariant and its definition of "committed". The
row-level policy admitting the function's rows is keyed to that
recorded access via the administrative session context ADR-018 §5.5 defines; no `BYPASSRLS`, no owner
bypass, no superuser anywhere in the path, and `FORCE ROW LEVEL SECURITY` on every table read — the
administrative roles are subject to the bypass prohibitions of ADR-018 §4.4 in full. ADR-018 owns the
predicate and setter form; this record owns that the admin path uses them, and binds to the
administrative setter form ADR-018 §5.5 defines:

- `app_ctx.record_admin_event()` — a `BEFORE INSERT` **trigger function** on `audit.event`, owned by
  `pennilogic_ctx`, `EXECUTE` granted to no role, so the only way to extend its list is to insert an
  event; it appends the inserted event's id **and subject** to the MAC-bound, transaction-local list
  `app.recorded_admin_events`.
- `app_ctx.begin_admin_access(p_audit_event_id, p_request_id, p_acr)` — `EXECUTE` only for
  `admin_api_owner`; denies unless the event id is present in this transaction's verified recorded
  list; takes the subject **from that list entry**, never by re-reading a row; sets the actor to
  `admin:<audit_event_id>`, `app.admin_event`, `app.admin_subject` and `app.acr = p_acr` — the
  administrative `acr` value the policy point verified (§7), passed explicitly.
- `app_ctx.begin_redaction(p_unmask_grant, p_request_id, p_acr)` — `EXECUTE` only for
  `redaction_service`; denies unless `audit.confirmation_status(p_unmask_grant)` reports an active,
  chain-confirmed grant; sets the actor to `redaction:<grant>` and the context from the grant.
- Policy family `admin_recorded_access` — `TO admin_api_owner, redaction_service USING owner_id =
  verified_admin_subject()`; it is the only policy under which either role sees a customer row.

Administrative functions **never set the customer principal context** — an administrative role that
could assume `app.principal_id` (ADR-018 §5.2) would be impersonation, not administration — and no
administrative role ever receives `app.principal_id`; the customer setter is not executable by any
administrative role (§4.1). The semantics layered on that setter form — masked views, four-eyes,
audit and unmask — are this record's (§4.2, §5, §6, §8). `SECURITY DEFINER` does not bypass
row-level security here — the research pitfall applies to a definer that owns the table or holds
`BYPASSRLS`, and `admin_api_owner` is neither: it defines no customer table and is not the table
owner whose privileges row-level security would exempt. Every `admin_api`-schema definer function
sets an explicit `search_path`, has `EXECUTE` revoked from `PUBLIC` and granted only to `admin_app`,
takes typed parameters and builds no SQL from input; the other definer functions this record names
keep the grantees of §4.1 (`begin_admin_access` → `admin_api_owner`; `begin_redaction` and
`audit.unmask_grant` → `redaction_service`; `audit.confirmation_status` → `admin_api_owner` and the
`T-PLT-05` broker identity) under the same `search_path` and no-`PUBLIC` rules; a lint over the
catalogue enforces this (§13).

#### 4.4 What every administrative read or effect must record

In the foundational event (and therefore in the chain): `audit_event_id`; operator principal id and
administrative session id; action (closed vocabulary, §10); subject id (null for system-level
actions); resource type and id; data classes or column groups read or changed (never the values);
whether the read was masked or unmasked; access-basis reference and its provenance class; purpose
code; approver principal id and the approver's signed assertion when one is required; the co-present
principals of a break-glass session; policy bundle version and decision; the `acr` value verified;
time-box bounds; origin — the operator network allowlist entry and the client-certificate fingerprint,
both opaque identifiers resolved in the infrastructure inventory, never a raw address or a human
name; outcome and reason code; row or record count. Never a monetary value, a merchant string, raw
message content, a credential or key material — enforced by a schema test with planted values
(`T-SEC-03`).

### 5. Policy evaluation point

1. **One point, in-process, default deny.** The admin API has a single middleware through which every
   route — including health, static and framework routes — passes before any handler. The decision is
   computed from a policy bundle versioned in the `api` repository and deployed with the artefact; a
   policy change is a reviewed deploy, not a runtime edit. A route with no explicit allow rule is
   denied; a route added without a decision fails a coverage check and the build; an evaluation error
   or timeout denies (fail secure). Requests without an administrative principal never produce one
   trail row per request: the edge answers load-balancer health probes on its own listener; the admin
   API's health route is the single allow rule for an unauthenticated caller (the platform's probe
   addresses only) and emits a metric, not an event; the console serves static assets only inside an
   authenticated operator session, where an asset fetch names no resource and is counted on the
   session, not recorded as a decision; and any other unauthenticated request that reaches the policy
   point is denied and recorded as one `system`-actor `admin.request.unauthenticated` event per
   (route, reason, minute) carrying a count — chain-scoped like every `admin.*` event (§8.1), which is
   cheap at that volume and is itself evidence of scanning.
2. **Decision inputs**: operator principal, role and current elevation; the `acr` value of the
   operator delegation token (§7); session age and idle time; action and resource; subject;
   access-basis reference with provenance class and its verification result; purpose code; approver
   identity and assertion; policy bundle version; chain health (§8.4); the operator's rolling
   distinct-subject counter. Every decision that names an administrative principal, allow or deny,
   is a foundational event with an outcome and a reason code, and the refusal response carries its
   `audit_event_id`.
3. **Explicit allow rules** grant the smallest scope that a named capability needs. Deterministic
   caps are policy, not analytics: an operator who exceeds the published distinct-subject cap within
   its rolling window is refused, not flagged, however many sessions they open (§16).
4. **Elevation** is time-boxed to the published maximum, requires a distinct approver who satisfies
   separation of duties with both the requester and the access-basis originator, expires mid-session,
   and issues no elevated session until its chain record is confirmed. Self-approval is impossible by
   construction (the approver field cannot equal the requester or the originator). An approval is a
   **hardware-factor assertion by the approver over the request's `audit_event_id`**, stored as the
   chain-scoped field `approver_assertion` of the approval event. The party that verifies it is
   **never the process that benefits from it**: the chain verifier (§8.5) — an infrastructure-zone
   identity the admin API, the relay and the redaction service cannot influence — checks every
   approval event's assertion against the administrative identity domain's **read-only credential
   registry** (the public keys of enrolled administrative credentials, exposed by the identity
   provider on a read endpoint reachable only from the infrastructure zone, §3.1 item 2) before it
   confirms the chain record, and writes `audit.approval_invalid` instead of `audit.chain_confirmed`
   when the assertion does not verify or was signed over another `audit_event_id`. Because every
   release in §8.4 item 3 waits for that confirmation, the same independent check protects unmask,
   elevation, export, broker sessions and break-glass alike; a compromised admin API that inserts a
   request and a garbage approval through its own functions obtains no confirmation and therefore
   nothing. The admin API may verify an assertion itself for a fast refusal, but that check is
   never the trust anchor. Only the verifier holds the registry path; the releasing processes rely
   on confirmation, which they observe through `audit.confirmation_status` (§8.4).
5. **Bulk export** is a formally requested job with two such approvals, executed by an export
   identity that is neither the admin API nor the relay, released only after both approval events are
   confirmed (§8.4), obtains plaintext only through the redaction service under the export's own
   chain-confirmed grant, subject by subject (it holds no key permission and no direct `SELECT` on a
   ciphertext or monetary column, §6.2), writes its output only to a governed location, and records
   one event per included subject (§8.2). The artefact is encrypted at rest under a key that neither
   the console nor the admin API holds; it expires within the published window (§16); every download
   is a chained `admin.export.download` event; its deletion is recorded; and it is never re-imported
   into any administrative surface (`T-ADM-05`).
6. **Break-glass** is an administrative principal whose credential is vaulted and used only with dual
   presence. Its allow rules cover **system control only** — kill switches over customer-facing
   features and AI providers, revocation of **customer** sessions and suspension of **customer**
   accounts — and **never** unmask, export or lifecycle grants; administrative session revocation,
   administrative suspension and every lifecycle operation are `T-ADM-06` operations excluded from
   every break-glass allow rule, so an incident cannot be used to lock out approvers or responders.
   No allow rule, break-glass or otherwise, targets the audit trail, the relay, the chain, the
   verifier or the policy bundle itself. Every use is time-boxed to the published maximum, fully
   recorded with `break_glass = true`, alerts on start, rotates the credential automatically after the
   session and requires a recorded post-use review. Emergency human reads of production data are
   `T-PLT-05` broker sessions, not console break-glass. If approval infrastructure is unavailable the
   answer for an unmask is to wait: statutory clocks run in days, and ticket-system unavailability
   denies (`T-ADM-03`). Two distinct failure conditions have distinct outcomes: if the **trail** is
   unavailable, no action can commit (§8.1 transactional coupling) and the console offers nothing; if
   **projection or verification** is unhealthy, the five release-gated capabilities are denied while
   masked reads and system control continue (§8.4 item 4). Platform incident response runs through
   the deploy and rollback path (`T-PLT-01`) under `T-PLT-05` governance, never through the console.
7. **Administrative effects** (suspension, refund, quota override, plan or provider configuration,
   lifecycle changes) execute as `admin_api` functions that write the effect and its event in one
   transaction; the console's confirmation primitive records intended before/after values through the
   API before the change applies (`T-ADM-04`). Before/after values enter the event only for
   non-financial configuration (status, plan and feature rows); a money-bearing effect such as a
   refund or quota override records the reference to the billing or ledger record it created — which
   is itself append-only and server-authoritative — never the amount (§4.4). A money-bearing
   **intent** persists only as the pending billing or ledger request record (ADR-001 integer minor
   units, ADR-002 append-only) created in the same `admin_api` transaction and referenced by the
   event — never console-side and never in the event. The admin API never calls the customer-facing
   API and never uses `pennilogic_app`.

**Relationship to ADR-005.** ADR-005 decides relationship-based, default-deny authorization "rather
than roles" and names admin within its scope. Administrative decisions keep exactly that semantics:
requester, approver, access-basis originator, subject and co-present principal are distinct
relations the bundle evaluates, separation of duties is a relationship constraint, and role is a
coarse capability class that is never sufficient on its own. What this record fixes is the
**evaluation shape** for the administrative path only: the decision is computed in-process from the
deployed bundle, and the ReBAC engine ADR-005 names (OpenFGA or SpiceDB) is not in the administrative
request path — it is absent from the §3.1 egress list and is never consulted from the admin zone
(§3.2), because a remote decision point in the deny path converts its outage into an outage or an
open door. This narrows ADR-005's application to the administrative surface without replacing it:
customer-facing sharing continues to use the engine as ADR-005 and ADR-018 §5.4 describe, so
`supersedes` stays `null`.

### 6. Unmask path and key custody

#### 6.1 Only the approved path produces an unredacted value

Unredacted values never appear by default and are never a property of a role. An unmask is a distinct
operation (`T-CON-07`) that requires, all at once: an **eligible access basis** — a user-originated
support request verified open for that subject by the ticket-verification interface (`T-SUP-01`), a
system-originated security incident record, or a statutory request record — with immutable
originator, source channel and provenance class, where an **operator-originated record is never
eligible** regardless of reassignment or reopening; a **purpose code** from the published vocabulary;
a **distinct approver** eligible under the administrator lifecycle (`T-ADM-06`) who is neither the
requester nor the originator and whose approval is a hardware-factor assertion over the request
(§5 item 4); a **field-level scope** (subject, named fields, time range); a **time-box** with single
delivery; a **fresh hardware-factor assertion** by the requester; and **chain confirmation** before
release (§8.4).
The affected subject is notified through the notification outbox under its payload allowlist, and the
unmask appears in their transparency stream without operator identity (§8.6).

#### 6.2 Administrators never receive raw data encryption keys — enforcement

1. **Database grants**: `admin_app` and `admin_api_owner` hold no privilege on the wrapped-DEK
   tables ADR-018 names, and no `admin_api` view or function selects a ciphertext or key column.
2. **Key-management policy**: the administrative workload identities hold no decrypt or unwrap
   permission on any customer key-encryption key. Of the identities reachable from the
   administrative path, only the redaction service's identity in the customer zone holds unwrap
   permission (custody per ADR-018 §5.5; the customer API's own unwrap for serving users is
   ADR-018's and is not reachable from the administrative path). This is verified by an IAM policy
   test in every environment.
3. **The redaction service is the only read path.** After the authorizing event is recorded and its
   chain record confirmed, the admin API calls the internal unmask endpoint of the redaction service
   over mTLS with the `audit_event_id` alone. The service calls `audit.unmask_grant(audit_event_id)`,
   which returns the grant only if the request event has outcome `allow`, an approval event from a
   distinct principal exists, a **verifier-written** `audit.chain_confirmed` event covers both the
   request and the approval (§5 item 4, §8.4), the time-box is unexpired and no prior
   `redaction.delivered` event exists; it enters the row through `begin_redaction(grant, request_id,
   acr)` (ADR-018 §5.5), then unwraps the subject's DEK in its own process, decrypts **only the named
   fields of the named record**, returns their plaintext, and writes a `redaction.delivered` event
   with the same correlation. The admin API receives field values, never a key, never a whole row,
   never more than the approved scope, never twice for one grant.
4. **Code boundary**: the admin console's import boundary refuses every PenniLogic package
   (`T-SCA-ADM-01`); the `admin-api` module has a static assertion that it imports no key-unwrap
   client and a runtime assertion that no administrative path unwraps a key (`T-ADM-03`).
5. **Break-glass cannot unmask** (§5 item 6), so no emergency mode widens key access.
6. **No human key path.** The guarantee holds for people as well as processes: a `T-PLT-05` broker or
   break-glass human session never carries KMS unwrap or decrypt permission, never combines a
   wrapped-DEK row with custodian access, and never exports key material; key-custodian operations
   are the separate dual-control path of ADR-018 §6.1.1, which yields no plaintext DEK or KEK to any
   human; and the standing-access inventory asserts that no human-assignable role or broker session
   template carries a decrypt or unwrap permission (§4.1, §13 item 2).

### 7. Administrative identity domain

The administrative identity domain is a **separate realm, tenant or instance** of whatever identity
provider ADR-019 selects for customers — or a different provider — with these properties, none of
which ADR-019 may relax: its own issuer and audience, relying-party identifier, user store, session
store, credential store and recovery process; no federation with the customer realm; enrolment only
of organisational identities through the joiner flow (`T-ADM-06`), which requires a **device-bound,
phishing-resistant FIDO2 credential with user verification** before the first privileged action;
synced or cloud-backed passkeys are not accepted for administrative identities; **no customer
credential of any kind** (passkey, OTP, password, recovery code) is accepted, and the customer realm
accepts no administrative credential — the admin API validates only the administrative issuer and
audience, the customer API rejects them. An administrative principal is a distinct identifier
namespace with no link to a customer id, bound to exactly one natural person who holds at most one
active administrative principal (enforced at enrolment, `T-ADM-06`), so one person cannot be both
requester and approver under two administrative names; an operator who is also a customer holds two
unrelated principals, and the administrative domain never reads or writes the customer identity
store. The **break-glass principal is the one administrative principal not bound to a natural
person**: its use records the two co-present persons' own principals on the `break_glass = true`
session event (`actor_principal_id` is the break-glass principal; `co_present_principal_ids` names
both people), it is never an eligible approver (§5 item 4) and never a requester of unmask, export or
lifecycle (§5 item 6).

The **operator delegation token** the admin service presents to the admin API (ADR-019 §13, `aud =
pennilogic-api-admin`, `act.sub = admin`) carries the administrative principal (`sub`), the
administrative session id (`sid`), the administrative issuer, `acr`, `iat`, `exp` and a unique `jti`
— and nothing else: access-basis, purpose and approver references are operation parameters recorded
in the event (§4.4, §10 item 2), never token claims, so a session is never coupled to a support
artefact. ADR-019 §13's "ticket reference … as ADR-020 specifies" resolves to **no ticket claim**.
This record owns the values and lifetimes of `sub`, `sid` and `acr`; ADR-019 §13 owns the envelope's
**replay bounds**, which the admin API enforces on every request exactly as ADR-019 states them:
`exp` ≤ 120 seconds, `jti` unique and cached for the token lifetime with reuse rejected, and a
**revocation check of the administrative `sid`** against this record's administrative session store
— so a session revoked by a mover or leaver flow (`T-ADM-06`), by idle or absolute expiry (§16) or by
break-glass response is refused on the next request even while its token is unexpired; an omitted,
expired, replayed or revoked-session token is a denial recorded as `admin.auth.*` (§5 item 2).
Under the same ADR-019 §13 dispatch rule this record registers the additional service identities and
audiences of §3.1 item 2 (`admin-api` and `export-job`; `pennilogic-api-ticket-verify`, `wl+jwt`
from `admin-api` only; `pennilogic-redaction-unmask`, `wl+jwt` from `admin-api` or `export-job`). The
administrative **`acr` vocabulary** this record defines — distinct from ADR-019's customer values — is
`admin:hw_fresh` (a device-bound hardware-factor assertion verified within the freshness bound of
§16) and `admin:hw_session` (the hardware factor verified at sign-in, older than the bound). The policy
point verifies the token, evaluates `acr`, and passes the verified value to `begin_admin_access`
(§4.3): unmask, elevation, export, lifecycle and break-glass require `admin:hw_fresh`; masked reads,
aggregate reads and non-financial configuration accept `admin:hw_session`; no other value exists.

Administrative authentication is itself audited: the admin API writes `admin.auth.*` events for what
it observes (token accepted or rejected, audience or issuer mismatch, `acr` insufficient), and the
administrative identity provider's authentication log — sign-in success and failure, FIDO2 enrolment
and reset, lockout, issuer-side session revocation — is forwarded into the trail under the `idp.*`
namespace by the `idp_ingest` role (§4.1), which the chain covers (§8.1), so credential stuffing or
enrolment abuse against the administrative realm is chained, alertable from the trail (§12) and
visible to the two-way reconciliation.

Administrative sessions have the published idle and absolute limits, are revocable mid-session, and
every unmask, elevation, export and lifecycle action requires a fresh hardware-factor assertion.
Mover and leaver flows revoke sessions, refresh families, database role membership, console access
and break-glass eligibility with evidence; standing permissions lapse automatically when not
recertified within the published window; an approval by an ineligible or offboarded principal is
refused (`T-ADM-06`).

### 8. The three audit streams

#### 8.1 Purpose, reader, store

| Stream | Purpose | Written by | Read by | Store | Write |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Foundational application audit trail** (`audit.event`, `T-SEC-03`) | System of record for the existence, order and content of every security-, consent-, sharing- and administration-relevant action; the transactional outbox for the other two streams | The action's own transaction, by whichever role performs it (`pennilogic_app`, `admin_api_owner` functions, `redaction_service`, broker sessions, `idp_ingest`, and the verifier for `audit.*`) | Security and compliance reviewers through governed `T-PLT-05` queries; the relay and the verifier; **never** a subject-facing endpoint (§8.6) | Primary Postgres cluster, dedicated `audit` schema, `INSERT`-only, `FORCE ROW LEVEL SECURITY`, partitioned by time | **Single authoritative write**, atomic with the action it describes; an action without its event rolls back and vice versa |
| **Chained tamper-evident administrative log** (`chain.record`, `T-ADM-02`) | Unfalsifiable record of every administrative, break-glass, broker and administrative-authentication action for insider-risk investigation, dispute and regulator evidence | The relay's chain projector, as `chain_writer`, appending a hash-chained record per chain-scoped event; confirmed by the verifier | Security reviewers and the DPO through governed queries; the audit-review console screen (`T-ADM-11`) in masked form through `chain_reader`, each read itself recorded; the verifier with independent credentials; administrators can read, never write or delete | **Own store**: a **separate PostgreSQL cluster** (`audit_chain`) — its own hosts, superuser set, credentials, backups and WAL, administered under an identity that is neither the primary-cluster path nor the admin or relay identities — continuously streamed to a **write-once object copy** under a platform-enforced, compliance-mode retention lock in a third identity boundary (§8.5) | **Projection** of the trail: at-least-once, idempotent by `audit_event_id`, strictly ordered, single appender enforced by lease and unique constraints (§8.4) |
| **Subject-visible transparency stream** (two tables: `transparency.entry` for operator-, system- and self-actor entries, and ADR-018's `access_events` for household-member cross-user-read entries; one payload contract, `T-CON-09`) | Shows a data subject what happened to their data and account: what was accessed or changed, by which class of actor, under which purpose, when — and, for household members, by whom (ADR-005) | The relay's transparency projector, as `audit_relay`, by a **positive projection** into a closed schema — including `access_events`, which `pennilogic_app` never writes (ADR-018 §1.2, §4.2) | The subject only, under RLS, through the transparency payload contract on the client trust surfaces (`T-TRU-02`, `T-TRU-03`, `T-TRU-04`); a subject's transparency history reaches the subject through the `T-CON-09` export path. **No administrative role reads it** (§4.1): the rights-request operator queue (`T-ADM-13`) reads request metadata and writes chained `admin.*` events, never a transparency table; an operator read of a subject's transparency history is not a capability this record grants — if a later record adds one, it is a recorded `admin.transparency.read` event admitted only under `admin_recorded_access` and itself projected to the subject | Primary cluster: `transparency.entry` and `access_events`, RLS `owner/subject = current subject` (`owner_rows` on `access_events`, ADR-018 §4.2); writable only by the relay's projection policies and by the ADR-021 erasure job's single step (§4.1, §8.7); **shares the cluster** with the trail because it is derived and rebuildable from it, and holds nothing the trail does not | **Projection** of the trail by its own consumer and cursor (§8.4), with the subject notification enqueued in the same transaction as the entry |

**Chain scope is one decidable predicate on the `action` field alone:** an event is chain-scoped
**iff** its namespace is `admin.*`, `redaction.*`, `broker.*` or `idp.*`. Nothing else enters the
predicate: `audit.*` events — confirmations, approval verdicts, verification results and checkpoints
— are written by the verifier about the chain and are not chain-scoped, so confirmation never
recurses; `auth.*`, `consent.*` and `sharing.*` are customer-actor events that stay in the trail and,
where subject-visible, the transparency stream. The actor-class invariant is a consequence enforced
by a schema constraint, not a second predicate: an event whose actor class is `operator`,
`break_glass_operator` or `production_broker` must carry a chain-scoped namespace (`T-SEC-03`).

**`access_events` is a projection, not a fourth stream and not a second write.** ADR-018 §1.2 and §4.2
define `access_events` — `id`, `viewer_id`, `owner_id`, `category_id`, `detail_level`,
`policy_identifier`, `request_id`, `audit_event_id`, `occurred_at` — as the cross-user-read entries
of this record's transparency stream. The model is exactly §8.4 item 1: for every grant-mediated read
`pennilogic_app` inserts **one** foundational `audit.event` in the `sharing.read.*` namespace, in the
grantee's transaction, whose typed `sharing.*` columns carry those fields (§10 item 1) and whose `FOR INSERT` policy carries the
integrity check ADR-018 §1.2 requires, scoped to the read actions (§4.1) — the only write; the relay's
transparency projector then projects that event into `access_events` (`id = audit_event_id`, UUIDv7),
which the data subject reads under `owner_rows`. `pennilogic_app` never writes the projection, the
former direct-append form is withdrawn (ADR-018 §4.2), the viewer can neither read nor delete it, and
one event yields **one** transparency row — the `shared_detail_read` entry lives in `access_events`,
not additionally in `transparency.entry`. A grantor's own `sharing.grant.*` and `sharing.group.*`
events are self-actions: they carry `subject_id = actor_principal_id`, pass the policy's lifecycle
branch, and project as `self_action` into `transparency.entry` (§8.2, §13 item 11). The `T-CON-09`
payload unions the two tables into one subject-visible view.

#### 8.2 Events that appear in more than one stream

| Event class | Trail | Chain | Transparency (`access_kind`) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unmasked read of a subject's fields (console unmask, broker session read) | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ as `unmasked_read`, actor class `operator`, no operator identity |
| Account-affecting administrative action on a subject (suspend, reinstate, refund, quota override, rights-request fulfilment, erasure execution) | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ as `account_action` |
| Export job containing a subject's data | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ as `export`, one entry per included subject |
| Masked single-subject lookup | ✓ | ✓ | — (not detail-level; ADR-005's visibility rule attaches to detail access) |
| Policy decision (allow or deny), elevation request/approval/expiry, lifecycle, break-glass session, configuration change with no subject, administrative authentication (`admin.auth.*`, `idp.*`) | ✓ | ✓ | — |
| Household member's detail-level read of shared data (`sharing.read.*` event) | ✓ | — | ✓ as `shared_detail_read`, **one row in `access_events`** (`viewer_id` = the member's opaque principal reference, `policy_identifier` = the admitting grant policy; ADR-005; §8.6) |
| Subject's own authentication, consent and sharing-grant lifecycle actions (`auth.*`, `consent.*`, `sharing.grant.*`, `sharing.group.*`) | ✓ | — | ✓ as `self_action`, actor class `self`, in `transparency.entry` |

The transparency projection is the only subject-visible read path; the "events about themselves"
query in `T-SEC-03` is served from the two transparency tables and never from `audit.event`, because
the trail carries operator identity and internal reason codes.

#### 8.3 Correlation identifier

The identifier that correlates one administrative action across all three streams is
**`audit_event_id`**: a UUID version 7 (RFC 9562, time-ordered) minted once, server-side, by the
foundational audit module inside the action's transaction. It is the primary key of `audit.event`,
carried verbatim as `audit_event_id` in `chain.record`, in `transparency.entry`, as `id` and
`audit_event_id` in `access_events` (§8.1), in every response of the administrative contract
(`T-CON-07`), in the transparency payload (`T-CON-09`), in the broker session schema (`T-PLT-05`) and
in the unmask call (§6.2). A database write into any stream without it is rejected.
Approvals, confirmations and verdicts reference the event they concern through `related_event_id`
(trail and chain only). Because the identifier is opaque and the chain has no subject-facing read
path, a subject can quote it in a grievance and the DPO can locate the administrative record without
the record ever being exposed to the subject.

#### 8.4 Write semantics and failure behaviour

1. **No dual write anywhere.** The only write performed inside an action's transaction is the
   foundational event. Neither the chain nor the transparency store is written by the acting
   process. A two-store commit would need two-phase commit or produce phantom or missing records on
   partial failure; both are rejected.
2. **Outbox, two projectors, one appender.** `audit.event` is the outbox. The relay runs **two
   independent consumers with separate cursors** over it, so the chain never waits on transparency:
   the **chain projector** appends the chain record and streams it to the write-once copy; the
   **transparency projector** writes the transparency row — into `transparency.entry` or, for a
   `sharing.*` cross-user read, into `access_events` (§8.1); one consumer, one cursor, both tables —
   and enqueues the subject notification in one transaction. Each cursor is gap-safe (it must be proven not to skip an event whose transaction
   commits after a higher sequence value) and advances only after its own writes commit. Delivery is
   at-least-once; every projection is idempotent by `audit_event_id`; projection failure or lag
   **never loses an event** because the event is durable in the trail. A transparency mapping failure
   (a `data_classes` member with no positive mapping, a schema rejection) alerts and dead-letters that
   event for the transparency side only — the trail still holds it, the chain still records it — and
   never trips the health precondition; only chain, copy or confirmation failures do. The chain
   projector is a **single appender by enforcement, not assertion**: it holds a lease (a database
   advisory lock on the chain cluster) for the duration of its run, `chain.record` carries unique
   constraints on `seq` and on `audit_event_id`, and streaming to the copy happens only after the
   local append has committed, so two relay instances during a rolling deploy cannot both append or
   stream a conflicting record.
3. **Chain-before-release, confirmed by the verifier.** The one place a second store's state matters
   is release of something sensitive. An unmasked value, an elevated session token, an export
   artefact, a **production broker session** (`T-PLT-05`) and a break-glass session are released only
   after their authorizing event — and, where one is required, its approval event — has been
   confirmed. Confirmation is a trail event, `audit.chain_confirmed` with `related_event_id` pointing
   at the confirmed event, that only `chain_verifier` can write (§4.1): the verifier tails the chain
   cluster continuously, and confirms a record only when its hash links to the previous record, the
   write-once copy holds the identical record, the record's trail event exists, and — for an approval
   event — the `approver_assertion` verifies against the credential registry (§5 item 4); otherwise it
   writes `audit.approval_invalid` or raises the tampering alert of §8.5. The relay cannot write a
   confirmation, so a compromised relay can stall or forge but cannot open the gate; the admin API
   cannot write one either. The acting process observes confirmation through
   `audit.confirmation_status` and never through chain credentials of its own; `T-PLT-05` refuses to
   open a broker session until `audit.chain_confirmed` covers its `broker.session.request` event. If
   confirmation does not arrive within the published bound, the release is **refused**: a `*.refused`
   event is written with the same correlation, nothing is compensated because nothing was released,
   and the operator is told to retry later. A masked lookup or configuration change is not held on
   confirmation, because its event is already durable and it releases no protected value.
4. **Health precondition.** While chain-projection lag or confirmation lag exceeds its bound, or the
   last full verification is not green within its interval, the policy point and the broker deny the
   five release-gated capabilities — unmask, elevation, export, broker session, break-glass —
   outright (fail closed) and continue to allow masked reads and system control. Transparency lag
   alerts but does not deny.
5. **Authority.** The trail is authoritative for whether an action happened and what it recorded. The
   chain and its write-once copy are authoritative in an **integrity dispute** about an
   administrative action: where trail and chain disagree, the write-once copy is presumed correct for
   the investigation and the disagreement is itself a security incident. The transparency stream is
   never authoritative and is rebuildable from the trail **against ADR-021's erasure tombstones** by
   re-running the projection: the projector consults the ADR-021 erasure register (the journaled
   erasure events and the `T-SEC-07` tombstones) and re-applies the single erasure step of §8.7 as it
   replays, so a rebuild never resurrects an erased subject's rows or un-pseudonymises a `viewer_id`
   that the register says was rotated; a full re-projection after an erasure reproduces exactly the
   post-erasure state (§13 item 12).

#### 8.5 Chain verification, time and recovery

Each `chain.record` carries `seq`, `audit_event_id`, the canonical event bytes (RFC 8785 JSON
canonicalization of the chain-scoped fields, which include `approver_assertion` and exclude values by
construction), `prev_hash` and `record_hash = SHA-256(prev_hash ‖ canonical_bytes)`; the first
record's `prev_hash` is 32 zero bytes. **`seq` is the sole ordering authority** of the chain; time
never orders it. `occurred_at` and every time-box comparison (§5, §6) use the database server clock
inside the acting transaction (`transaction_timestamp()`), never a client or console clock; every host
that writes or verifies a stream synchronises to the time source the jurisdiction profile requires
(`T-CMP-04`; the CERT-In research baseline is NIC/NPL NTP), and the verifier reports an `occurred_at`
regression between consecutive records beyond the published tolerance (§16) as a finding.

The verifier tails the chain continuously for confirmation (§8.4 item 3) and, at the published
checkpoint interval, signs the current head (`seq`, `record_hash`, time) with a key only its identity
holds and writes the checkpoint to the write-once copy and to `chain.checkpoint`. Full verification
recomputes every hash from the last verified checkpoint, compares the chain cluster with the
write-once copy record by record, re-verifies every stored `approver_assertion` since the last
checkpoint, reconciles chain records against trail events by `audit_event_id` in both directions (a
chain record without a trail event is a forged projection; a chain-scoped trail event without a chain
record after the lag bound is a lost or suppressed projection), reconciles every `admin.*` session id
against an `idp.*` sign-in event for the same principal within the session lifetime (a session with no
independent sign-in is a fabricated identity, §11), and publishes the result, chain length
and last verified position — **as trail events** (`audit.verification.completed`,
`audit.verification.checkpoint`, `audit.verification.mismatch`), so a second reader can spot-check the
verifier itself; until Gate C's external anchoring exists, that independent spot check is the operator
red-team suite and the console quality gate (`T-ADM-12`, `T-QA-13`). Any mismatch alerts within the
published interval.

Administrators cannot delete from the chain: `chain_writer` has `INSERT` only, no login role has
`UPDATE`/`DELETE`, the write-once copy is under a retention lock that the platform enforces against
every principal including the account owner, and the verifier's credentials are held by neither the
admin process nor the relay. The write-once copy must meet these requirements, whatever platform
ADR-022 selects: the lock is applied **per object at write time** with the §8.7 chain period; a
governance-style lock that an account root or owner can shorten or lift does **not** qualify — only a
compliance-mode lock that no principal can shorten; shortening or removing the lock fails for every
principal (§13 item 7); the copy is versioned and replicated; and it is resident where the
jurisdiction profile requires (`T-CMP-04`). Because confirmation — and therefore every release-gated
capability — depends on the copy, the copy is in the release path with two further requirements: the
chain projector treats a record as streamed only on a **durable-put confirmation** (the platform's
acknowledgement that the object is committed to redundant storage with its lock applied, not a
buffered accept), and the verifier's **read-back** of the copy runs under an availability objective
at least as strict as the admin API's own (`T-PLT-03`); a copy that cannot be read back within the
confirmation bound fails closed through §8.4 item 4, never open.

**Recovery.** The write-once copy *is* the durable backup of the chain up to the last streamed
record, and the trail holds every chain-scoped event, so recovery has a defined source and no
improvisation: (1) restore the chain cluster from the write-once copy to its last record; (2)
re-project the remainder from the trail — canonical bytes are deterministic, so a re-projected tail
yields identical hashes and **no discontinuity** when they match the copy's expectations; (3) rewind
the chain projector's cursor to the restored head as part of the same approved change, so the relay
neither skips the lost tail nor double-appends; (4) re-establish the verifier from the last signed
checkpoint and run a full verification before confirmations resume. Only a **lossy** restore — one
whose recovered head cannot be re-projected to match — records an explicit `chain.discontinuity`
referencing the approved change record, accepted only when a signed checkpoint precedes it; anything
else is tampering. `T-ADM-02` owns the recovery procedure and its RPO/RTO with `T-PLT-02`, and the
restore drill of §13 item 7 is a release condition for Gate A.

#### 8.6 Transparency isolation

The transparency stream is built by a **positive projection**: an explicit mapping from named
foundational fields into the closed transparency schema (`T-CON-09`) — `transparency.entry` for
operator-, system- and self-actor entries and ADR-018's `access_events` for household cross-user
reads (§8.1). It is never produced by filtering the chain or the trail with a deny-list, so a new
event field cannot leak by default. `transparency.entry` carries `audit_event_id`, `occurred_at`,
`actor_class`, `access_kind`, `data_classes`, `purpose_code` with the purpose registry version (§10
item 3) and nothing else; `access_events` carries ADR-018 §1.2's fields, whose `viewer_id` is the
household member's **opaque principal reference** (`viewer_id`, surfaced as `actor_ref` in the
payload), never a stored
display identity. **Resolution of `viewer_id` is anchored to the row, not to current grant state:**
the reading surface resolves it to the member's display identity through the **admitting grant
recorded on that row** — its `owner_id`, `viewer_id`, `category_id`, `detail_level` and
`policy_identifier` name the relationship under which the read was admitted — so a later revocation,
safe exit or re-grant never changes what the subject sees about a past read, and a member who has
since left still appears as the person who viewed; only that member's ADR-021 erasure changes the
row, through the single erasure step stated in §8.7. `viewer_id` is the single permitted other-user reference in the payload, an explicit narrowing
of `T-CON-09`'s prohibition (§10 item 3). The stream never carries an operator, break-glass or broker
identity, a ticket or incident reference, an internal reason code, a network origin, a policy
version, a row count, a value or key material; `operator`, `break_glass_operator` and
`production_broker` all project to `operator`. No subject-facing service has any read path to
`chain.record`, so the chained log cannot be reader-filtered into the transparency stream by accident,
and a schema test with planted operator identity, ticket reference and reason code fails the build.

The entry is **always written**; the one thing that may be deferred is its visibility. A **notification
hold** is keyed to the **purpose** recorded on the unmask or account action — one dimension, never
the access basis's provenance class, which answers a different question (who originated the basis,
§6.1) and plays no part in holds: purpose `statutory_request` → a hold recorded by counsel from the
jurisdiction profile (`T-CMP-04`); purposes `security_incident` and `account_safety` → a hold recorded
by the security lead, a principal distinct from the requester, the originator and the approver, so
that a fraud or security investigation is not tipped off; every other purpose (`support_request`,
`billing_dispute`, `rights_request`) is visible and notified immediately, whatever its provenance
class. A hold is itself a chained `admin.*` event with a mandatory expiry within the published maximum
(§16); it can neither be open-ended nor originate from the requesting operator; renewal before expiry
is a fresh chained hold event with its own expiry and the same origination rules; and at expiry the
entry and its notification become visible with no further action and **no exception** — an
unavailable counsel or security lead at expiry does not extend a hold.

#### 8.7 Retention, personal data and erasure

Retention values and the classification of what is personal data are read from the counsel-reviewed
jurisdiction profile (`T-CMP-04`), not asserted here. What this record fixes is the structure the
profile applies to.

**Personal-data classes in the streams.** The trail and the chain hold personal data of two kinds —
about the **subject** (`subject_id`, action, data classes, purpose, time: an identifiable person's
account events) and about **staff** (`actor_principal_id`, `approver_principal_id`,
`co_present_principal_ids`, origin allowlist entry and certificate fingerprint) — held for statutory
record-keeping and insider-risk evidence; the transparency stream holds the subject's own record and,
in `access_events`, the household member's opaque `viewer_id`. The classes, their lawful bases and
their post-erasure treatment are the profile's to confirm; this record does not call them "not
personal content".

**Why the fields survive erasure.** Every stream-borne identifier is **opaque by construction**: a
subject UUID resolves to nothing once ADR-006/ADR-021 erasure has destroyed the DEK and tombstoned the
`users` row; an administrative principal id, a certificate fingerprint and an allowlist entry resolve
only through the administrative identity directory and the infrastructure inventory, whose resolution
tables carry their own retention under `T-ADM-06` and `T-PLT-05`; `viewer_id` resolves at read time
through the admitting grant recorded on its row (§8.6). No stream holds a name, address, value,
message or credential (§4.4). Erasure therefore never rewrites the **trail or the chain**; it removes
the ability to resolve their identifiers. **The one erasure step on the transparency projections**,
stated here and nowhere else: the ADR-021 erasure job, on the same journaled erasure event, (i) deletes
the erased subject's own rows from `transparency.entry` and `access_events`, and (ii) rotates the
erased identifier to the erasure pseudonym ADR-018 §6.7 defines in `viewer_id` on counterparties'
`access_events` rows, so the counterparty keeps "an erased account viewed this" and nothing more;
these are the only writes other than the relay's inserts that either table ever receives (§4.1), the
`T-SEC-07` restore replay repeats them, and the projection rebuild of §8.4 item 5 re-applies them from
the erasure register.

**Periods.** (a) The **chain** and its write-once copy are retained uniformly for the greater of the
profile's security-log floor and its financial-action floor (research baselines 180 days and seven
years), because a hash chain cannot expire individual records without breaking continuity; the
uniform period is acceptable precisely because chain-scoped fields are opaque identifiers, vocabulary
codes and hashes, and the profile may require the resolution tables — not the chain — to be purged
earlier. (b) The **trail's** live partitions keep at least the security-log floor and are archived —
and the archive verified — before any partition is detached; the trail archive carries its **own
write-once lock equal to the security-log floor**, distinct from the chain's period, so customer
`auth.*`, `consent.*` and `sharing.*` rows are never locked for the financial floor. (c)
**Transparency** rows live as long as the subject's account plus the profile's floor, subject to the
erasure treatment above. (d) Post-retention pseudonymisation of subject identifiers, where the
profile requires it, is a `T-CMP-01` job on detached trail partitions and transparency rows, never on
the chain, whose identifiers are already unresolvable after erasure.

are what makes the capability exist at all.

### 10. Contracts and schema consequences

This record is the contract input for the artefacts below; they consume it and do not restate it.

1. **Audit event schema** (`T-SEC-03`, `api`): required fields `audit_event_id` (UUIDv7),
   `occurred_at` (UTC instant per ADR-015, database server clock), `actor_class`,

   (closed, namespaced vocabulary), `resource` (type, id), `outcome` (`allow` | `deny` | `error`),

   `purpose_code` (with purpose registry version), `approver_principal_id`, `approver_assertion` (the

   `policy_version`, `data_classes`, `masked` (boolean), `record_count`, `origin` (allowlist entry

### 11. Security and privacy consequences

**Of the chosen option.** A compromised customer surface reaches no administrative capability, and a
compromised administrative account reaches masked metadata of one subject at a time, with every step
recorded in a store it cannot alter; consistent falsification of an administrative action requires
compromising the primary cluster, the separate chain cluster and a compliance-locked object store
under a third identity, plus the verifier's signing key. Subjects learn of every detail-level access
without learning who the operator was. Cost: two extra deployables, one extra database cluster, a
write-once store, a two-consumer relay and a continuous verifier to operate; unmask, elevation, export
and broker sessions carry a bounded wait for verifier confirmation; when projection or verification is
unhealthy, support and platform work slow down by design; there is no emergency path to unmask, so
some incidents wait for approvers. Residual risks, each with its detection: a colluding requester,
originator, approver and — for a hold — security lead (four distinct eligible principals, all
recorded and subject-visible at the latest when the hold expires); a **compromised admin API
process**, whose reach is stated exactly: it connects as `admin_app`, so it can call every
`admin_api` function — a masked, non-financial single-subject lookup for **any** subject, one
committed event per subject, and every non-financial configuration effect (plan, feature, provider,
status rows) — and it can fabricate the operator principal, session id and `acr` it records on those
`admin.*` events, because the database cannot verify a token; it **cannot** obtain an unmasked value,
an elevated session, an export artefact, a broker session or a break-glass session (each waits for a
verifier confirmation it cannot write, §5 item 4), cannot read a ciphertext, monetary or key column,
cannot reach `audit.event`, `transparency.entry`, `access_events`, `admin_api.staged_result` or the
chain, cannot forge a `redaction.*`, `broker.*`, `idp.*` or `audit.*` event, cannot set the customer
principal context, cannot obtain a masked result without a committed recording event (the
stage-and-fetch invariant of §4.3 makes read-then-rollback, same-transaction fetch and
savepoint tricks yield nothing, and a stage is fetchable only by the principal and session that
recorded it), and cannot exceed the
distinct-subject cap **for any one operator principal**, which the `admin_api` fetch function
enforces a second time **inside the database** from the trail (a `pennilogic_ctx` status function
counts that principal's recent committed `admin.lookup.*` events), so the in-process cap is not the
only one; because it can fabricate the operator principal it records, it can widen its reach by
rotating principals — that rotation is **detected, not prevented**: every `admin.*` session id must
correspond to an `idp.*` sign-in event for the same principal, written independently by the identity
provider's forwarder, and the verifier reports the mismatch (§8.5) while the anomaly signals of §12
alert on it. A **compromised relay**, which can
stall (the health bound denies the five release-gated capabilities) or forge chain records (the
verifier refuses to confirm them and reconciliation reports them) but cannot write a confirmation, the
trail or the write-once copy; a **compromised verifier**, which holds the only checkpoint signing key
and could confirm a tampered chain or publish "green" while tampering exists — detected by the
verification events it must itself write into the trail (§8.5) and by the independent spot check of
`T-ADM-12` / `T-QA-13`, and closed structurally only by Gate C's external anchoring; a platform-level
compromise of the retention lock (outside this record's control; detected by verification divergence,
not prevented); metadata in the trail (opaque subject and staff identifiers, action codes) remains
readable to governed reviewers and is covered by `T-PLT-05` rather than by encryption; and fixed
cohort functions still carry differencing risk, reviewed per function.

**Of the rejected options.** In-application administration makes one customer-side flaw a full
cross-tenant financial read and every staff credential a total compromise. Direct console database
access reads plaintext balances past every control. A shared identity realm turns customer recovery
into administrative takeover. Dual writes lose or invent chain records on partial failure and force
the acting process to hold chain credentials. Deny-list filtering leaks new fields to subjects by
default. A remote policy service converts its outage into either an outage or an open door. Letting
the relay or the releasing process confirm its own chain record makes the four-eyes evidence
self-attested.

### 14. Rollout and rollback

Adopted on merge. `T-SEC-03` freezes the event schema against §10 before any authentication endpoint
emits an event; `T-ADM-01` starts from §3 to §5 and §7; `T-ADM-02` implements the relay — **both
projectors, including the transparency projection and the notification enqueue** — the chain cluster,
the verifier and the recovery procedure from §8.4 and §8.5, while `T-SEC-03` owns the projection's
schema tests; `T-PLT-05` from §4.1, §8.3 and §8.4; `T-ADM-06` the `idp.*` forwarding and the `acr`
enrolment rules of §7; the contract tickets from §10. Reversing any part of this record requires a
superseding ADR that states the migration cost: moving administrative routes into the customer
process would require re-issuing every database grant, re-homing the policy bundle and re-proving
every absence test; changing the write semantics would invalidate the chain's continuity and force a
signed discontinuity; changing the correlation identifier would break the link between every
existing transparency entry and its administrative record.
